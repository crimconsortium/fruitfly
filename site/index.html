CRIMCONSORTIUM

A fruit fly looks for criminology it can read

We dropped a simulated fruit fly brain onto a randomly chosen criminology paper and let it walk outward through reference lists. Open-access papers are corridors. Paywalled papers are walls. It explores until it runs out of things it can read.

An illustration of the idea, not the simulation. The measured run is below.

Where this came from

On 3 September 2026, about a hundred scientists at HHMI Janelia, the University of Cambridge, the MRC Laboratory of Molecular Biology and Google Research published the first complete connectome of an adult male Drosophila central nervous system in Cell: 166,700 neurons and 11,710 neuron types, every one of them traced. Google posted about it and it went round the internet. The data itself, MaleCNS v1.0, had been out since June under CC-BY, so anyone could download a whole fly brain.

People did, immediately. Within a week the fly was playing Doom and Super Mario 64, walking around Minecraft, and driving a car in a simulator. We pointed the same trick at something we care about.

Criminology is largely paywalled

This is measured, not assumed. Matthew Ashby checked 12,541 articles across 97 criminology journals published 2017–2019 and found that 22% were available to non-subscribers — in his words, “about half that found in other disciplines, even though authors had the right to make articles open without payment in at least 95% of cases.” More than three quarters of the field was hidden. He also found that Taylor & Francis, the largest publisher in criminology, had the lowest open share of any publisher — and his study appeared in a Taylor & Francis journal, where the version of record is behind the paywall and the readable copy is the manuscript he archived himself.

Our own index of the field agrees: of 30,654 articles in 51 journals, 31% are open under the rule below, 35% if you count bronze. So roughly two papers in three are a wall. The fly is just a way to walk through that.

How we did it

The corpus is the Web of Science research area Criminology & Penology, adopted as-is: 51 journals, 30,654 articles. European Journal of Criminology and Crime Science are not in it. That is Clarivate's boundary, not ours — and the authoritative version of that list is available only to Web of Science subscribers, so the conventional definition of criminology is itself paywalled. Titles, reference lists and access status come from OpenAlex, CC0.

Sampling. We drew 100 seed papers at random from that corpus — no curation, no hand-picking a paper with a good reference list. The fly is dropped on the seed and walks outward through reference lists, one paper at a time, never revisiting. A run ends when nothing readable is left next to it, or at a cap of 800 papers.

The brain. From the MaleCNS v1.0 flat connectome we keep the 1,342 descending neurons — the ones that carry commands out to the body — plus two hops of their strongest upstream partners, capped at 20,000 neurons and 1,016,133 signed connections out of the 151.9 million in the source. Connections weaker than five synapses are dropped, and each is made excitatory or inhibitory from its predicted neurotransmitter; 22,764 with no usable prediction are dropped. It is a real subgraph of a real fly, not the whole fly, and we say so.

The decision. Leaky integrate-and-fire neurons, 1 ms steps, 50 ms of spiking per decision, membrane time constant 20 ms. Each unvisited neighbour drives one of eight bands of input neurons at a rate set by its citation count; paywalled neighbours drive a band too, so they disturb the dynamics without ever being selectable. The descending neurons form eight readout groups and the candidate whose group fires most wins, ties broken by the random number generator. Gain is calibrated to a mean rate of 9.93 Hz. Nothing learns, nothing about the fly changes between steps, and the seed is fixed, so any run reproduces exactly.

What the run above found

The run above is one draw from those 100 seeds.

The video shows both sides of that: on the left, spikes moving through the real wiring; on the right, the single paper the fly is standing on and every reference it senses from there, each one marked open or paywalled. Almost all of them are paywalled. That is the whole finding — the fly is not clever and does not need to be, because what stops it is not the difficulty of the literature but the wall in front of it.

One run is one run

A different seed gives different numbers, so we ran all 100 of them. 75 of the 100 randomly drawn papers were paywalled at the seed: the fly could not take a single step. Of the 25 that let it start, the median run read 158 papers and bounced off about 2,000 walls — and 20 of those 25 hit our 800-paper cap, so their totals are floors, not ceilings. Across all 100 seeds it read 4,022 papers and hit 69,102 paywalls: about 17 walls for every paper it could open, roughly 5% of everything it touched. The individual numbers move from run to run. The finding does not.

We checked whether the brain mattered

Most of the fly-brain demos going around do not run a control. We did: the whole pipeline was rebuilt on degree-preserving shuffles of the same connectome, and we compared how well the readout identifies which input channel was driven. One shuffle would be an anecdote, so we run several and compare against their spread.

Everything behind this is open, which is the least we can do given the subject: the code, every trace, the corpus and this control live at github.com/crimconsortium/fruitfly — code MIT, our data and figures CC-BY 4.0.
